Every role comes with a set of repeating responsibilities that, when handled well, keep your work on track and your team aligned. This guide covers the most common daily and weekly tasks you will encounter regardless of department or seniority level — from how you start your morning to how you ask for help when something goes wrong. Build these habits early and they will become second nature within your first month.

## Daily Check-In

Begin each working day with a brief self-check before diving into tasks. Open your calendar to review scheduled meetings and deadlines. Scan your inbox and team chat for urgent messages that arrived overnight. Finally, glance at your to-do list and confirm your top three priorities for the day. This three-minute routine prevents surprises, keeps you proactive rather than reactive, and signals to teammates that you are available and engaged.

## Managing Your To-Do List

A reliable task list is the backbone of personal productivity. Use your company's designated task management tool — or a personal notebook if your team has no shared system — and keep it updated in real time.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Capture Every Task Immediately">
    The moment a new responsibility lands in your inbox, meeting notes, or chat, add it to your task list before doing anything else. Trying to remember tasks mentally leads to dropped balls. Write it down first, then decide when and how to act on it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Assign a Priority and a Due Date">
    Label each task as high, medium, or low priority and attach a realistic due date. If a task has no external deadline, set your own so it does not linger indefinitely. Review and adjust priorities at the start and end of each day.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Break Large Tasks into Subtasks">
    Any task that will take more than two hours to complete should be broken into smaller, actionable subtasks. This makes progress visible, reduces procrastination, and makes it easier to hand off work if you are unexpectedly unavailable.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Close Out Completed Tasks">
    Mark tasks complete as soon as you finish them. A regularly updated list gives you an accurate picture of your workload and provides a clear record of your output — useful during performance reviews and project retrospectives.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Submitting Reports or Updates

Many roles require regular progress reports, status updates, or data submissions. Follow these steps to make sure your submissions are accurate, on time, and easy for recipients to act on.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Gather Your Data and Notes">
    Before drafting any report, collect all relevant information: metrics, completed milestones, blockers, and decisions made since the last update. Check that your numbers are current and sourced from the agreed system of record.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Use the Standard Template">
    Most teams maintain a shared report template to ensure consistency. Locate the template in your team's shared drive or knowledge base, duplicate it, and fill in only the clearly marked fields. Never restructure a shared template without first consulting your manager.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review Before Sending">
    Proofread your report for typos, check that all figures match your source data, and confirm the report is addressed to the correct recipients. If your update contains sensitive information, verify that the distribution list is restricted to those who need it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Submit Through the Correct Channel">
    Send the report through the designated channel — whether that is email, a project management tool update, a shared document comment, or a dedicated reporting platform. Confirm submission and note the timestamp in your records.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Requesting Support

Knowing when and how to ask for help is a professional skill, not a weakness. When you encounter a blocker, start by checking the Job Knowledge Base and your team's documentation. If the answer is not there, message a knowledgeable colleague or post in your team's help channel. When raising a support request, always describe what you were trying to do, what you expected to happen, and what actually happened — this context allows others to assist you quickly and accurately.

<Warning>
  Escalate immediately — bypassing normal channels if necessary — when you encounter a security incident, a data breach or suspected data loss, a compliance violation, a health or safety risk, or a situation that could result in significant financial or reputational harm to the organization. Do not wait for a scheduled meeting to surface these issues. Contact your manager, IT security, or your company's designated emergency contact right away.
</Warning>

## Attending Meetings

Meetings are most valuable when every participant arrives prepared and engaged. Apply these best practices to make every meeting count:

- **Confirm the agenda beforehand.** If no agenda has been shared 24 hours before the meeting, ask the organizer for one. Knowing the topics in advance lets you prepare relevant questions and data.
- **Be on time.** Join or arrive at least one minute before the start time. Arriving late wastes everyone's time and signals disrespect for colleagues' schedules.
- **Take your own notes.** Do not rely solely on shared meeting notes. Write down action items assigned to you, key decisions made, and follow-up questions in your own notebook or task manager.
- **Speak up constructively.** If you have a relevant perspective, share it. Active participation leads to better decisions and builds your visibility within the team.
- **Confirm action items before the meeting closes.** Before leaving, verify who owns each next step and when it is due. This prevents ambiguity and keeps projects moving forward.
- **Follow up promptly.** Complete any action items assigned to you within the agreed timeframe and update the relevant project channel or shared doc so stakeholders can see progress.

<Tip>
  Batch similar tasks together to minimize context switching and preserve deep focus time. For example, block a 30-minute window each morning to process emails, another slot for report submissions, and a dedicated afternoon block for focused project work. Grouping like-for-like tasks reduces the mental overhead of switching modes and consistently adds up to hours of recovered productivity each week.
</Tip>
