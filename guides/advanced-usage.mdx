Once you have mastered the fundamentals, the next step is to work at a higher level of impact — not just executing tasks, but shaping how work gets done around you. This guide is for professionals who are already comfortable with day-to-day responsibilities and want to optimize their performance, strengthen cross-functional relationships, share their knowledge with others, and actively improve the tools and resources their team relies on.

## Optimizing Your Workflow

Sustainable high performance comes from deliberate system design, not just harder effort. Apply these techniques to reduce friction and consistently produce your best work.

**Time blocking with protected focus sessions.** Divide your calendar into distinct zones: one block for deep, focused work (writing, analysis, complex problem-solving), one for communications and quick tasks, and one for meetings. Guard your deep-work block aggressively by silencing notifications and setting your status to "Do Not Disturb." Even two uninterrupted hours a day compounds into significant output over a week.

**Automate repetitive actions.** Identify tasks you perform the same way every time — formatting reports, sending status updates, archiving completed files — and automate them using your platform's built-in workflow rules, macros, or integrations. The upfront investment of 30 minutes to create an automation often saves hours every month.

**Maintain a weekly review habit.** Set aside 20 minutes at the end of every Friday to review what you completed, what carried over, and what is coming up next week. Adjust your task priorities, clear your inbox to zero, and update any shared project boards. A consistent weekly review prevents backlog buildup and keeps your commitments visible to yourself and your team.

**Use templates for everything repeatable.** Any document, message, or report you produce more than twice deserves a template. Store your templates in a dedicated folder in your cloud drive and share them with your team. Well-designed templates save time, enforce consistency, and make it easier to onboard new team members to your processes.

## Cross-Team Collaboration

Working effectively beyond your immediate team multiplies your impact and accelerates project outcomes. Strong cross-functional collaboration requires intentional habits and clear communication.

Start by mapping the teams your work touches most frequently — this could be product, finance, legal, marketing, or engineering depending on your role. Identify one or two key contacts in each team and invest time in building genuine working relationships before you need something from them. A brief introduction message or a short virtual coffee chat goes a long way toward establishing goodwill.

When collaborating across departments, always align on a shared definition of success before any project kicks off. Different teams often measure outcomes differently; agreeing on shared metrics upfront prevents conflict later. Document decisions, responsibilities, and deadlines in a shared space — a project brief, a shared doc, or a dedicated channel — so there is no ambiguity about who owns what.

Proactively communicate progress and blockers to cross-functional partners, not just your direct manager. If a dependency is at risk, surface it early. The professionals who earn the most trust across an organization are those who raise issues before they become emergencies and who follow through on every commitment they make.

## Mentoring and Knowledge Sharing

As you grow in your role, sharing what you know becomes one of your most valuable contributions. Mentoring colleagues and documenting your expertise creates a multiplier effect — your knowledge continues to help others long after a single conversation.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify Who Would Benefit from Your Guidance">
    Think about newer team members, colleagues transitioning into adjacent responsibilities, or peers who have expressed interest in skills you have developed. Reach out proactively rather than waiting to be asked. A simple message offering to answer questions or walk someone through a process you know well is all it takes to start a mentoring relationship.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Structure Your Knowledge-Sharing Sessions">
    Prepare before each session with a clear agenda. Focus on transferring understanding, not just instructions — explain the "why" behind the processes and decisions you share, so the person you are mentoring can adapt your approach to new situations on their own.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Create Reusable Resources">
    After any knowledge-sharing conversation, write up the key points in a short document, checklist, or annotated example and share it in your team's shared drive. Written resources extend the reach of your guidance to colleagues who were not in the room and serve as a reference the mentee can return to.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Provide Ongoing Feedback and Check-Ins">
    Schedule a brief follow-up with anyone you have mentored two to four weeks after your initial sessions. Ask how they have applied what they learned, where they are still encountering friction, and what additional support would be helpful. Iterative mentoring leads to far deeper skill development than a single session ever can.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Contributing to the Knowledge Base

The Job Knowledge Base is only as useful as the information it contains — and the people closest to the work are best positioned to keep it accurate and comprehensive. If you notice a gap, an outdated article, or a process that is not yet documented, take the initiative to contribute.

To suggest a new article or an update to an existing one, open the relevant page in the Knowledge Base and use the feedback or edit button (visible to logged-in users with contributor access). Write a brief description of what is missing or incorrect and, where possible, draft the proposed content directly. Clear, specific submissions are reviewed and published much faster than vague requests.

If you want to propose an entirely new topic or section, submit your suggestion through the designated content request form in the HR portal or reach out to the Knowledge Base owner listed in the [Resources](/reference/resources) section.

<Note>
  All content contributions go through a lightweight editorial review before publication. A Knowledge Base editor will check your submission for accuracy, clarity, formatting, and alignment with the documentation style guide. Most reviews are completed within three to five business days. You will receive a notification when your contribution is published or if the reviewer has questions. Repeated contributors may be invited to join the editorial working group and help shape the Knowledge Base's long-term content strategy.
</Note>

<Tip>
  Build a personal toolkit — a curated collection of templates, checklists, reference docs, and browser bookmarks — that captures everything you reach for regularly. Store it somewhere instantly accessible, like a pinned note or a dedicated folder in your cloud drive. Sharing your toolkit with your team (and inviting them to contribute their own best resources) transforms a personal efficiency habit into a collective one, raising the performance floor for everyone around you.
</Tip>
