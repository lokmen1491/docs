A workflow is a defined sequence of steps that guides a task or process from initiation to completion. Workflows exist to reduce ambiguity, maintain quality, and ensure that everyone involved in a piece of work knows what to do, when to do it, and how their contribution connects to the larger goal. When you follow established workflows consistently, you minimize errors, reduce duplicated effort, and make it easier for teammates and managers to track progress and provide support.

## Types of Workflows

### Onboarding Workflows

Onboarding workflows cover everything that needs to happen when someone — or something — is new. This includes welcoming a new team member, setting up access and tools, completing required training, and confirming that the person understands their initial responsibilities. Onboarding workflows are typically time-boxed, with clear milestones at the end of the first day, first week, and first month. Following this workflow carefully ensures new starters become productive quickly and do not miss compliance or security requirements.

### Daily Operations Workflows

Daily operations workflows capture the recurring tasks that keep your work running smoothly — things like checking in on active projects, responding to requests within agreed response times, updating task statuses, and participating in team syncs. These workflows are usually lightweight by design, but their consistent execution is what prevents small issues from becoming large problems. Treat your daily operations workflow as a reliable checklist that anchors your workday.

### Project Delivery Workflows

Project delivery workflows govern how a body of work moves from kickoff to completion. They typically include stages such as scoping and planning, execution, review and quality assurance, stakeholder sign-off, and handover or launch. Each stage has defined entry and exit criteria so everyone knows when it is appropriate to move forward. Adhering to this workflow ensures projects are delivered on time, within scope, and at the expected level of quality.

### Escalation Workflows

Escalation workflows define what to do when something goes wrong or exceeds your authority to resolve independently. They specify the conditions that trigger an escalation (for example, a missed SLA, a client complaint, or a technical blocker), who to notify, what information to include, and the expected response timeline. Knowing and following the escalation workflow means issues get resolved faster and the right people are involved at the right time.

## How to Follow a Workflow

<Steps>
  <Step title="Identify the correct workflow for your task">
    Before starting any significant piece of work, confirm which workflow applies. Check the relevant section of the Job Knowledge Base, your team's internal documentation, or ask your manager if you are unsure. Starting with the right workflow saves time and prevents rework.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Read through all steps before you begin">
    Scan the entire workflow once before taking action. This gives you a complete picture of what is involved, surfaces any steps that require preparation or input from others, and helps you anticipate potential blockers early.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Gather required inputs and resources">
    Many workflows depend on specific information, approvals, or tools being available before you can proceed. Collect everything you need upfront — access credentials, templates, stakeholder sign-offs, or data — so you can move through the workflow without unnecessary interruptions.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Execute each step in order, documenting as you go">
    Work through the workflow sequentially, completing each step fully before moving to the next. Record your progress, decisions, and any deviations in the appropriate tracking tool so there is a clear audit trail and colleagues can pick up where you left off if needed.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Confirm completion and communicate outcomes">
    Once all steps are done, verify that the workflow's completion criteria have been met. Notify relevant stakeholders, update any tickets or project boards to reflect the new status, and archive or file any documentation produced during the process.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Customizing Workflows

<Note>
  Most workflows can be adapted to fit the specific context of your role, team, or project — but adaptation is not the same as elimination. When customizing a workflow, you may add steps that reflect your team's particular tools or standards, reorder steps where your context genuinely requires it, or delegate certain steps to appropriate colleagues. What you should never do is remove a step simply because it feels redundant or time-consuming. If you believe a step is unnecessary, raise it with your manager or team lead so the workflow can be formally reviewed and updated for everyone.
</Note>

<Warning>
  Never skip required steps in a workflow, even under time pressure. Required steps exist to protect quality, ensure compliance, and maintain accountability. Skipping them — even once — can create downstream problems that are far more costly to fix than the time saved. If you are uncertain whether a step is required or optional, treat it as required until you receive explicit confirmation otherwise.
</Warning>
