Best practices are not rigid rules — they are well-tested habits and approaches that consistently produce better outcomes across a wide range of roles and situations. Adopting them reduces friction, builds trust with colleagues and stakeholders, and frees up mental energy you would otherwise spend solving avoidable problems. The practices below reflect what high-performing professionals do day in and day out, and they are designed to complement the workflows and task guides elsewhere in the Job Knowledge Base.

## Communication

<Accordion title="Be clear and concise in every message">
  Before sending any written communication — email, chat message, or document comment — ask yourself whether the recipient has everything they need to understand your point and take action. State the purpose of the message upfront, keep the body focused on one topic, and end with a clear indication of what (if anything) you need from the other person. Avoid burying the key request or decision at the bottom of a long message.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Choose the right channel for the right conversation">
  Use asynchronous channels like email or project management comments for non-urgent updates, detailed information sharing, and decisions that need a written record. Use synchronous channels like chat or a quick call for time-sensitive issues, nuanced discussions, or anything likely to require back-and-forth. Defaulting to the wrong channel creates unnecessary noise and delays.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Acknowledge receipt and set expectations">
  When someone sends you a request or question, acknowledge it promptly — even if you cannot provide a full answer immediately. A brief "Got this, I'll respond by end of day" is far more helpful than silence. Setting clear expectations about your response timeline prevents follow-up chasers and demonstrates reliability.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Give and receive feedback constructively">
  When offering feedback, focus on specific behaviors and outcomes rather than character or intent. Frame suggestions around what could be improved and why, and offer concrete alternatives where possible. When receiving feedback, listen fully before responding, ask clarifying questions if needed, and separate your initial emotional reaction from the substance of what is being said.
</Accordion>

## Time Management

<Accordion title="Plan your day the evening before or first thing each morning">
  Spend five to ten minutes each morning — or the evening before — identifying your top three priorities for the day. Having a written plan before your inbox and notifications demand attention helps you stay focused on high-value work rather than reacting to whatever surfaces first. Use a simple task list, calendar block, or your team's project tool to capture the plan.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Group similar tasks to reduce context switching">
  Every time you switch between unrelated tasks, your brain needs time to reorient — a cost that adds up significantly over a workday. Batch similar activities together: process all your email in one or two dedicated windows, conduct all your review tasks back-to-back, and hold meetings in blocks rather than scattering them throughout the day. This approach preserves long stretches of focused time for complex work.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Protect time for deep, focused work">
  High-quality output on complex tasks requires uninterrupted concentration. Block time on your calendar specifically for deep work, and during those blocks silence non-urgent notifications and let colleagues know you are heads-down. Even one ninety-minute deep work session per day can substantially increase the quality and volume of work you produce.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Review and adjust your commitments regularly">
  At the end of each week, look back at what you committed to and what you actually completed. If you consistently over-commit, practice reducing the number of items on your daily list or breaking large tasks into smaller, more realistic units. If you regularly finish early, look for opportunities to take on stretch work or support colleagues. Regular review keeps your capacity estimates accurate over time.
</Accordion>

## Documentation

<Accordion title="Write things down at the moment decisions are made">
  Memory is unreliable, especially across weeks or months. Capture decisions, rationale, and action items immediately — during or immediately after the conversation in which they occur. A brief note in the relevant project thread, meeting notes doc, or task description is sufficient. Future you, and your teammates, will thank you for the habit.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Use consistent naming and filing conventions">
  Adopt a clear, shared naming convention for files and folders so documents are easy to find without having to ask who has them. Include a date, project name, or version number in file names where relevant. Store documents in the agreed team location rather than only on your local machine or personal drive, ensuring access is not lost when you are away or change roles.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Keep documentation current, not just complete">
  Documentation that is out of date can be worse than no documentation at all, because it misleads people who trust it. Build a habit of updating the relevant document whenever a process, decision, or piece of information changes. Mark documents with their last-reviewed date so readers know how fresh the content is.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Write for your audience, not yourself">
  When creating documentation for others, start by identifying who will read it and what they will need to do as a result. Use plain language, define jargon the first time it appears, and structure the document so it is easy to scan. Avoid dense paragraphs of background context when bullet points or numbered steps will serve the reader better.
</Accordion>

## Collaboration

<Accordion title="Clarify roles and ownership before work begins">
  Ambiguity about who is responsible for what is one of the most common causes of dropped balls and duplicated effort. At the start of any shared task or project, agree explicitly on who owns each deliverable, who needs to be consulted, and who simply needs to be kept informed. Use a RACI model or equivalent if your team has one, or document ownership directly in the task or project brief.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Share progress proactively, not just when asked">
  Do not wait to be chased for updates. Regularly share where you are on shared work — especially if you have hit a blocker, changed your timeline, or made a decision that affects others. Proactive updates keep teammates aligned, prevent duplicate work, and build a reputation for reliability.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Offer help before you are asked">
  Notice when a teammate is overloaded, stuck, or approaching a deadline, and offer specific assistance rather than a generic "let me know if you need anything." Concrete offers — "I can draft that section by tomorrow" or "I have capacity to review that before you send it" — are far more likely to be accepted and useful than open-ended availability.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Resolve disagreements directly and promptly">
  When a conflict or disagreement arises, address it directly with the person involved rather than letting it fester or venting to others. Approach the conversation with curiosity rather than defensiveness — your goal is to understand their perspective and find a workable solution, not to win. If you cannot reach resolution together, involve your manager or a neutral third party promptly rather than letting the tension linger.
</Accordion>

<Tip>
  Schedule a brief monthly review — even just fifteen minutes — to revisit these best practices. As your role evolves and your team's needs change, some habits may need reinforcing and others may need updating. Sharing what you are working on with a trusted colleague or your manager can also help you stay accountable and get useful perspective on your progress.
</Tip>
